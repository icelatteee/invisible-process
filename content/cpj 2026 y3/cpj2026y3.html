Week 1 — Making the Mechanism Visible

Where this started

In Year 2, I explored AI-assisted coding to understand agency and authorship. After finishing the paper, I realized I was far more curious about the interaction process itself.

That curiosity grew out of a second brief I ran: the first asked designers to follow a fixed reference, but the second was completely open. The open brief yielded vastly more varied results, and that variation seemed to depend directly on how each designer uniquely negotiated their interaction with the AI.

Why Shenzhen

I took a trip back home to Shenzhen, expecting to see advanced tech and computing infrastructure. What actually surprised me was their prototyping speed. Materials like acrylic can be sourced locally in a day or two, allowing people to test ideas immediately without weeks of upfront planning being recognised more and more, at this scale. I also visited The 1st Shenzhen Art & Technology Biennale ("A Glimpse of the Infinite" at the new Shenzhen International Museum of Art / SIMoA,) The three-story exhibition featured international tech artists pushing creative boundaries. Seeing computational design recognized at this scale inspired me and proved how expansive this field really is.

Precedent: Illusionary Light by Yang Hongyan

My favorite piece was Illusionary Light by Yang Hongyan. At first glance, it appeared ethereal, with light illuminating the space in a delicate way. Looking closer, the setup was surprisingly simple: colorful acrylic shapes projected and reflected onto the wall by a visible mechanism. Without that exposed mechanism, the work wouldn't have been as compelling. It reminded me that complexity isn't required—careful design and intention are what matter. Just as the exposed mechanism was essential to Illusionary Light, I want the underlying process of interacting with AI to be part of the final work I display.

This is my final year to experiment before re-entering the industry, and I intend to make full use of it by taking risks on a process I genuinely enjoy.

Week 2 — Finding the Exchange

Where I was stuck

Preparing for the Week 2 presentation was frustrating because my topic felt completely overwhelming. It was far too big and vague, to the point where anything could belong to it. I didn't know how to funnel it down or pinpoint what I actually wanted to investigate.

How I got here

Everything traced back to my Year 2 work on agency and authorship in AI-assisted coding. Trying to slide-deck my project forced a fundamental question: What actually separates us as human designers from an AI? Is it thinking, depth, experience, or process? I kept landing on process and thinking.

My supervisors, Andreas and Kapi, pointed me toward 1960s cybernetics and computer art literature. Reading it was an eye-opener—the initial societal pushback against computers back then mirrors our current panic around AI:

  • 1960s (e.g., Taylor): Hostility around whether machine outputs counted as "art," and aggressive debates over authorship, while early pioneers saw an opportunity to explore new systems.
  • Today (e.g., Inie et al.): Fears around job displacement, loss of control, credit, and authentic human voice.

This gave me my foundational keywords: cybernetics, conversation theory, feedback loop, and sense-making.

The lightbulb moment was realizing that the final output matters far less than the internal process: the doing, reasoning, and negotiation of agency. Designers are trained to own a project from start to finish. "Vibe coding" with AI flips that completely. The creative process shifts from pure execution to finding a way to communicate with a machine to get what you want.

Works that Inspired me

Presentation Feedback Breakdown

  • Scope: Too broad. I agree—I need to set strict parameters and construct tangible tests.
  • Readings: Strong historical range (Burnham, Pask, Schön, Boden), but lacked contemporary AI and Research through Design (RtD) contexts. I've started reading into AI co-working literature to figure out how to extract structured data from these reflective experiments.
  • Historical References: Lecturers noted my references skew toward older media art. I think context matters—these historical frameworks give us a lens to understand current AI without reinventing the wheel.
  • The Feedback Loop: I realized I still couldn't explain a "feedback loop" beyond a basic ping-pong exchange (I input ➔ computer responds ➔ I respond). I need to ground this deeper.

What I now think

I used to think my project was about what AI produces and who owns it. Now I see it is about the exchange itself—that is where the reasoning, control issues, and struggle actually happen.

I also have a working hypothesis: human conversation inherently has friction because both sides clarify and push back, whereas interacting with AI often feels frictionless or overly compliant. But is friction the same thing as challenge, or am I mixing up two different mechanics?

Moving Forward

Define the parameters.

Find a way to explore the exchange by testing it, not only reading about it.

Week 3

My lecturer pointed me toward the AlphaGo documentary as a framework for understanding what it actually means to negotiate with a machine.

AlphaGo and Move 37

What fascinated me about AlphaGo was its underlying architecture—a system trained through reinforcement learning and self-play. DeepMind’s CEO described it as "a magical piece that can extend the power of your mind," raising an interesting question: how does a tool stretch our cognitive boundary without completely replacing human intent?

The game of Go relies on minimal rules—a grid, two colors, and territorial control—yet its move combinations create a near-infinite probability space. In Game 2 against Lee Sedol, AlphaGo executed Move 37—a low shoulder hit on the 5th line that human experts initially dismissed as a blunder because it violated centuries of opening theory, only to realize later that it reshaped the entire board strategy.

Credit: Image by David Silver et Al, AlphaGo Paper [1]

Research Relevance

Move 37 connects directly to my Pillar 2 argument: a collaborator needs deep grounding, but not so much that it flattens into an average, to produce transformational rather than merely exploratory creativity. AlphaGo was grounded in millions of iterations, yet because it was trained against itself rather than purely copying human historical games, it produced a move outside established human conventions.

Challenges Faced

I felt lost regarding the broader design context this week. I had always assumed "Interaction Design" was strictly confined to visual interfaces (UI/UX). Exploring Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) literature shifted my perspective: the mechanism of how we interact with an object or system is itself curated. Designing that interactive exchange—the parameters, boundaries, and feedback—is an act of design in its own right.

What I now think

  • On Machine Creativity: I used to think AI could only function within the exact limits we give it. Seeing reinforcement learning in AlphaGo showed me how an agent can generate transformational outcomes by discovering moves no human taught it to look for.
  • On Interaction Design: I used to view interaction design merely as building screens or physical installations. Now I realize that curating the dialogue and process between user and system is the real medium.

Moving Forward

This left me more curious about the different types of AI algorithms that exist — what the differences are, how we're using them, and what the best way is to use one if the goal is to spark creativity.

Building My First Agent

To understand agentic workflows in creative coding firsthand, I built a baseline AI agent.

Credit: Milestone AI

Rather than just answering prompts, an agentic system follows a goal-driven loop:

  1. Workflow: Plain-English instruction files defining objectives, constraints, and rules.
  2. The Agent: Evaluates inputs, decides next steps, and executes logic autonomously.
  3. Tools: Function calls that allow the system to read context, run terminal commands, or search external data.

Reflection Building the agent was a pure exercise in self-directed learning. I wasn't applying it to a studio project yet; I just wanted to deconstruct the architecture. Understanding the difference between a static prompt-response loop and an agentic workflow gives me a clearer technical mental model for how machine autonomy is constructed.

Week 4 — Body Language

Trigger / Reframe

Mind-mapping human-machine interaction this week brought me to an unexpected realization: what fundamentally separates collaborating with a machine from working with a human is the absence of body language. In my mapping, "gestures and motion" emerged as a missing sensory channel alongside code, text chat, speech, and visuals.

Experiment: Kerning, Round 2

Approach I built a baseline reinforcement-learning agent to kern letter pairs against a computed reward, manually overriding its adjustments across repeated rounds. I wanted to learn the mechanics of the algorithm, test what a system understands about a nuanced craft skill like typography, and observe what "working with" an AI actually feels like.

Process & Observations In the first pass, a squished preview box distorted my perception, causing the agent to set everything far too tight. Yet, that bug produced a surprising visual result because my own visibility was compromised. In the second pass, after fixing the display bug, the agent appeared to "fight back" against my adjustments.

Ultimately, I realized there was no genuine resistance:

  • The interaction lacked friction—the system simply calculated against modified inputs without understanding my intent.
  • I never understood why the agent chose specific values.
  • Because there was no shared dialogue, I defaulted to pushing my own judgment through rather than negotiating.

Comparing This to AlphaGo

The kerning agent never truly learned; it simply responded to the immediate constraints of each round. Unlike AlphaGo, which generated transformative breakthroughs like Move 37 by repeatedly playing against itself and refining its internal model over thousands of iterations, this agent remained completely static between rounds. There was no cumulative intelligence or self-correction happening under the hood—it didn't get smarter, more consistent, or more nuanced in its understanding of spatial relationships the more it was corrected.

When it "fought back" in round two, it wasn't displaying genuine resistance or novel stylistic conviction, but merely executing its fixed calculation logic against modified inputs. Because it couldn't build an evolving, grounded perspective from iterative feedback, its outputs never moved beyond basic exploratory adjustments into the realm of truly surprising or transformational design.

Feedback Breakdown (Consultation, Thursday)

Speaking with my supervisor on Thursday finally gave me some clear boundaries to anchor the project. The overall scope is officially locked across three core pillars: Interaction Design, AI-Assisted Creativity, and Creative Practices. I’m defining my domain of Interaction Design specifically through HCI for now, which gives me a much more grounded framework to evaluate human-machine collaboration.

However, the feedback on my recent experiment design hit hard. My supervisor kept repeating that the setup was way too serious and niche—that "not everyone would appreciate or understand it." Looking back, I can see where that came from. I got so caught up in setting up a rigid testing structure that I ended up stripping away the playfulness. Going into Week 5, I need to figure out how to pivot these experiments so they feel more accessible and engaging, without losing the critical inquiry underneath.

Moving forward, if I’m genuinely investigating AI as a collaborator rather than just a utility tool, I have to learn to catch myself when I try to force the outcome. I need to give the system enough room to push back, even if sitting in that uncertainty feels deeply uncomfortable.

Week 5 — Lost in Translation, Again"

Trigger

This week I wanted to dive deeper into what collaboration actually means, for me specifically, before testing it directly with AI.

Defining Collaboration

Defining Collaboration

What collaboration means to me: 1 + 1 = 100 — when two or more come together, the result should be far bigger than the sum of its parts. It is an active process that relies on feedback, real-time communication, unexpected outcomes, assisting each other, and allowing completely different ideas to collide.

Google defines it much more plainly: "the act of two or more people or organisations working together to create, complete, or achieve a shared goal."

Comparing my own definition against a standard dictionary definition brought up two core questions that I’m sitting with for this studio:

  • Does the computer actually have to be creative for us to collaborate? This feels like a live, open question rather than a simple gap to fill. If a system doesn't possess its own creative agency, can the interaction still count as a true partnership, or is it just an advanced form of delegation?
  • Are human experiences what make us creative? I think yes. Human experience allows us to feel, touch, and contextualise things through lived emotion and physical reality. That visceral layer adds a depth and dimension to creativity that a computer simply doesn't have—and might never be able to replicate.

Boden Re-Read

I went back to Boden's The Creative Mind to get a deeper understanding, since she explores computational creativity directly. Her distinction between H-creativity (historically novel, to all of humankind) and P-creativity (novel just to the individual, regardless of whether others got there first) shifted how I think about creativity — and sparked this week's experiments: let the AI work with me directly, and see if there's creativity there at all.

Two lines from the book stayed with me:

"Because creativity by definition involves not only novelty but value, and because values are highly variable, it follows that many arguments about creativity are rooted in disagreements about value."

"The answer to our opening question, then, is that there are many intriguing relations between creativity and computers. Computers can come up with new ideas, and help people to do so. Both their failures and their successes help us think more clearly about our own creative powers."

At the time, these felt like a point toward understanding what it means to be creative.

Experiment 1 — Grid System, with Gemini

Approach I asked Gemini to collaborate on a p5.js piece exploring a grid system, starting from a completely open brief.

Process & Observations Instead of starting simple, Gemini immediately dumped several heavy directions on me right out of the gate—suggesting motion dynamics, specific visual styles, and complex grid logics all at once. I paused and pushed back, asking why it chose that starting point. It gave a detailed technical breakdown: grids as constraint systems, cell resolution, local coordinate systems, and a "progressive scale" moving from uniform to parametric grids.

That answer triggered a realization. I asked it directly: "You've already given me an outcome I can only react to—how is that collaboration?"

It immediately reframed its approach and asked me to set the intent first. But that was the turning point: I realized I didn't actually have a specific idea in mind to create. The brief was far too open for me to bring anything grounded of my own to the table.

Experiment 2 — Button

Approach

After the open-brief paralysis in Experiment 01, I started much simpler and more deliberately this time: a p5.js button that spawns particles on click. I wanted to test whether setting a tight, hyper-specific constraint from the beginning would foster a genuine sense of collaboration rather than mere delegation.

Process & Observations

Gemini built a baseline featuring a particle burst and console logging feedback, which gave me a satisfying code structure to debug against. Instead of just letting the machine write everything, I jumped in and started editing the code directly—tweaking the canvas sizing, glow intensity, particle count, and color parameters until I landed on a soft, flowy aesthetic I envisioned as magic, ethereal, and space-like.

I then ran a test: I asked Gemini to inspect my code diff and guess what visual changes I had made and why, without giving it any verbal hints. It gave a remarkably confident technical breakdown, accurately tracing the adjustments in my code.

However, its interpretation of my vibe fell completely flat. It read my visual changes as "bubbly and energetic"—a far cry from the ethereal, feathery atmosphere I was attempting to build.

When we tried to negotiate a "middle ground," I resisted. Its suggested tweaks were pushing a narrative I simply didn't agree with. Its default understanding of "ethereal" was incredibly literal and storybook-like (think generic pastels and fairies), whereas my definition was rooted in fluid motion, lightness, and subtle weightlessness.

As we kept pushing parameters back and forth, the visual output briefly turned gross—resembling a chaotic nest of worms crawling out of the button. But the longer I stared at it, the form shifted. The accidental density and organic movement started to look like a living coral reef.

Challenges Faced & Reflection

This experiment brought several core realizations into focus:

  • Translation Loss as the Content: The AI cannot fully read my creative intent because it doesn't process reality the way a human brain does; it infers purely from code diffs and averages its predictions toward the most plausible response. Yet, that friction is precisely what makes working with it compelling. When my message breaks down in translation, what comes back is often an unexpected detour. This directly echoes my Year 2 Art by Telephone project—the message degrading across the channel isn't a bug; the breakdown itself becomes the actual content.
  • The Illusion of Surprise: The shift from "gross worms" to "coral reef" felt like a genuine moment of AI agency at first. But stepping back, I realized I had expected the machine to generate something entirely out-of-this-world, only to be handed something deeply familiar. It wasn't inventing a new aesthetic language—it was just recombining existing visual data.

This marks a clear pattern converging across my research: whether it's my kerning agent, Gemini on a grid, or particle systems on a button, current AI systems lack the grounded context to push beyond exploratory adjustments into truly transformational territory without flattening into an average.

Unresolved Question (Carrying Forward)

Is the gap between my aesthetic taste and the AI's output simply a prompt engineering issue—a matter of feeding it richer context (fixable)? Or is it fundamentally a "skill issue"—where the AI lacks the refined, experience-built taste, tactile nuance, and emotional intuition that a human collaborator naturally brings to the table? I'm intentionally leaving this open to test in my next iteration.

Moving Forward

Trigger: RPO Peer Review

The peer review session was a huge reality check. Hearing my classmates validate that they understood what I was trying to explore was a relief, but the feedback hit the exact same pain point: the overall scope is still way too big and vague for anyone to pinpoint what the core takeaway actually is. My three project pillars are now locked to give this inquiry a firmer structural spine:

  1. Interaction Design (focusing on HCI frameworks and human agency)
  2. Creative Coding (using p5.js as the tangible testing ground)
  3. AI-Assisted Creative Process (interrogating the friction, misinterpretation, and negotiation between human and machine)

Designing the Table & Exhibition Setup

During the feedback, a major question came up regarding the physical table setup for the exhibition: How do we help someone understand the process from start to end without dumping a wall of text on them?

Right now, the experiments exist as isolated code sketches and screen interactions, but an outsider walking up to the table won't see the internal dialogue, the pushback, or the visual misinterpretations that happened along the way.

Experiment 01: Understanding Feedback Loop

Approach I set up a basic, bare-bones feedback loop in TouchDesigner and applied a simple transform to it—moving the canvas along the x-axis with a Tile/Repeat operation.

Process & Observations I deliberately stripped away any complex visuals or generative effects to break the feedback loop down to its absolute simplest form. I wanted to observe what a feedback loop actually means when stripped of fluff, and how that concept translates raw onto a computer screen.

Reflection This was a purely sense-making exercise for me. Watching a frame repeatedly sample itself and step across the screen made the mechanics tangible. It wasn't about making a final visual piece; it was about grounding myself in seeing, in the simplest possible form, how a computer "understands" and processes recursion.

Experiment 02: Hand Tracking (Translating Gesture)

Approach To explore how a feedback loop looks and feels from the machine's perspective—in a way a human could actually perceive—I built a gesture-tracking system in TouchDesigner using computer vision.

Process & Observations I mapped a pinch gesture to calculate the spatial distance between my fingers, using a second hand to trigger the next gesture state. I used the pinch's hold value to drive the visual output directly, narrowing down the control range so the interaction felt tighter. Because raw tracking data is inherently noisy, I ran the values through a lag and smoothing filter to soothe the constant jitter coming off the camera feed.

Reflection & Challenges I genuinely enjoyed the feeling of a machine reading my body language. Even though I knew logically that the system wasn't "understanding" anything—just evaluating pixel matrices and coordinates—it felt like it understood what I meant. That felt sense of responsiveness is the most compelling part of the interaction. Being able to see that response visualised in real time was what made the experiment interesting, rather than the raw tracking technology itself. This exact moment is what got me interested in working with physical gesture more seriously as a medium for machine dialogue.

Experiment 03: Noise Grid (Visualising Data Fields)

Approach Instead of using Perlin noise the standard way—as an invisible force field to nudge or displace other objects—I used a particle system to physically plot where the noise values themselves sit in space.

Process & Observations I wanted to render the noise field itself visible. By placing particles directly at the coordinate values of the noise function, I translated an abstract math function into a physical, structural grid on screen.

Reflection I was curious how a raw string of floating-point numbers could translate into something physical I could actually look at. Seeing the data laid out in space allowed me to visualize and genuinely grasp what the computer was processing under the hood. The math stopped being an abstract black box and became a tangible landscape.

What I Now Think

Looking across all three experiments, the single most compelling element was the real-time feedback loop—the instant visual reaction on screen, which is so easy to overlook when working with digital systems. That immediacy is precisely what produced the sense of familiarity and connection I felt during the hand-tracking experiment. The instant response bridged the gap; it made the machine feel less like an unapproachable black box and more like a system meeting me halfway in space.

Feedback Breakdown — Andreas, "Meaning Finding"

My consultation with Andreas this week forced a hard pivot toward meaning-finding. He pushed me with a series of core questions: Why am I doing this? What counts as going deeper? What actually is a system? How do gesture and interaction differ? What does this do in terms of feeling and meaning? Where does this go next?

Sifting through those questions brought me back to a fundamental gap:

  • Why I'm doing this: There is a deep lack of mutual understanding between us and the machines we build. AI and algorithmic tools are permanently part of our design toolkit now, yet our day-to-day interactions with them still feel deeply awkward, clumsy, and misaligned.
  • Going deeper: For me, "going deeper" isn't about adding technological complexity—it collapses into solving that exact alignment problem. It’s asking how we can interact with a machine meaningfully, maintain our own creative agency, and still achieve genuine co-creation rather than just delegation.
  • System vs. Gesture & Interaction: A system is the sandbox—the set of rules, boundaries, and logic we program for the machine to operate within. Gesture is the physical body moving through space, while interaction is the dialogue that happens when that physical movement meets the computer's digital boundary.
  • Feeling & Meaning: The emotional weight comes back to that sense of familiarity and connection I noticed in the hand-tracking test. When a machine responds fluently to human nuance, the friction softens into dialogue.

Pillar Shift — Comparing to the Earlier RPO Draft

Looking back at my earlier RPO draft alongside this week's pillars reveals where my thinking is actually shifting—and where I might just be slipping into shorthand.

In the earlier draft, my three pillar titles were framed around critical friction and reflective practice:

  1. Interaction Design: Designing the Exchange, and Who Gets to Design It
  2. AI-Assisted Creativity: Two Ways an AI Collaborator Fails to Surprise You
  3. Creative Practices: Tacit Judgment as the Actual Site of Friction

This week, my list looks much simpler on the surface: Interaction Design, Creative Coding, and AI-Assisted Creative Process.

The first pillar (Interaction Design) holds steady as the overarching HCI framework. The second (AI-Assisted Creative Process) is mostly wording drift rather than a fundamental pivot.

The real structural shift is in the third pillar: replacing Creative Practices (which was rooted in tacit human judgment and internal friction) with Creative Coding (a medium-and-tool framing). This shift toward "Creative Coding as Building Your Own System" is where my later RPO draft ultimately settles, but seeing it emerge here forces a necessary question:

Was this week where that pivot actually happened, or was "Creative Coding" just loose shorthand while the real inquiry underneath remained about tacit judgment?

Tracing my trajectory over the last few experiments points to a genuine shift in my focus. Moving through the TouchDesigner hand-tracking setup and the Perlin noise grid pulled me away from passively critiquing what the AI outputs. The physical act of building the pipeline—smoothing jittery input values, mapping particle grids, and structuring real-time feedback loops—made the architecture of the system itself far more compelling than simply evaluating the AI's "taste."

Rather than sitting back and judging an black-box agent, my interest has moved to constructing the sandbox—designing the exact rules, constraints, and feedback loops that govern how machine and human meet.

Key Takeaways & Synthesis

  • Shift from Critique to Construction: The friction isn't just happening inside my head as a perfectionist user (tacit judgment); it is physically encoded into the system parameters I write (creative coding).
  • System as the Site of Agency: Building my own code environments gives me the agency I felt was missing in the open-brief Gemini experiments.
  • Open Direction: As I head into the next phase, the core challenge remains: how to build a creative coding system tight enough to give clear feedback, yet open enough to allow for genuine, unexpected co-creation.

Moving Forward

I know now that I’m interested in gestures. Moving into the next phase, I need to re-examine my RPO and restructure my current pillars to reflect that reality. But I’m still holding a set of fundamental questions open: What exactly am I interested in when it comes to gestures? Why gesture specifically, and how does it link back to my broader investigation of the man-and-machine process?
