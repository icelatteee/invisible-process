Trigger

What does writing mean?

Weeks 14 and 15 were independent study, with one ad-hoc consultation. Two notes started the week. First, I wrote that I had to specify which type of gestures I am looking at, intuitive ones, like waving a hand to mean something. Then the feedback at the consultation asked: what does writing mean here? What does a program mean? How can you use your body to develop a language, and how do we use our body to write? Look at how Hydra is built, and write a three-gesture program.

Prototype 1

Human language → gesture

  • Andie · triangle
  • Celine · rippling
  • Kimberly · circle
  • Roger · double

Idea. Charades: people understand each other through body movement alone. Before writing the full grammar, what gestures do people already use?

Why. To link the three languages together: human language, gestural language and programming language.

How. I broke down the categories p5.js uses most (objects, values, and animation or movement) and wrote prompt cards for each word: some obvious, some ambiguous like “blob”, some hard to sign like “quarter circle”. I filmed four people (Andie, Celine, Kimberly and Roger) signing each card, then showed the videos to others to guess. A precedent: Wobbrock et al. asked people to invent gestures for commands like “move”, “zoom” and “rotate” and found shared mental models (cited in Ashbrook and Starner 2160).

Prompt card explorations

I explored a few layouts for the prompt cards: each word set in a dotted, bitmap-like type, with its category and “Gestural Experiment 001” around it.

Making the cards

I wanted the prompt cards to recreate the punch-card effect I had been drawn to in Week 10, where every punched hole is a switch. So the final layout has a hole in each corner. I test-printed the layout, cut the cards out, and punched every hole by hand.

Guessing the gestures

After filming, I asked four people to watch the videos and write down what they thought each gesture meant, on a worksheet: Ashley guessed Andie's signs, Hannah guessed Roger's, Keegan guessed Celine's, and Casey guessed Kimberly's.

The worksheet, “Guess the Gestural 001”, has a signer and an interpreter, and three rows of six: objects, values and motion. That makes eighteen words, six for each part of a line of code.

  • Ashley
  • Hannah
  • Keegan
  • Casey

Some guesses landed: Ashley read the triangle, circle and rectangle exactly, and Keegan read the triangle and saw the rectangle as a square. Others went somewhere else entirely. Casey read Kimberly's objects as an up button, a church, a truck, a sweet potato, a parking sign and a pickle, and Hannah read one of Roger's gestures as a moustache.

Two things stood out to me while they were guessing. The interpreters would redo the motion themselves while writing down an answer, to see what their own brain would register. And different people approached it differently: those who are more whimsical, like Casey and Hannah, were more descriptive.

Reflection. Some gestures were consistent across everyone; others were harder to guess. But when I told people the answer, it instantly clicked. It is about the context.

Charades is a party game in which one player acts out a word or phrase without speaking while their team guesses it. It is a ready-made test of how much meaning a body can carry on its own.

Process

Choosing Hydra

This week I settled the system. The hand is tracked by MediaPipe, which recognises the sign. My first table turns the sign into a symbol, the symbols form a line, and my second table turns each symbol into a visual, run live in Hydra. AI only recognises the sign. Every meaning is mine: if I say a peace sign is a dot, it will be a dot. The users are designers and creative technologists who need some basic understanding to write the code, with a physical booklet to refer to.

I had first thought of Teachable Machine, to give the AI my own syntax and grammar, and asked whether it would let me control the step from gesture to syntax more. I kept MediaPipe. For the AI, I settled on it as an interpreter: it just registers the sign and translates it into the visual. I chose Hydra because its visuals are the closest to generative visuals, and because a new line can change what is already running. If a circle is already drawn and I want something else, I run a new line, where in p5.js I would have to go back into the code and edit it.

MediaPipe is Google's open-source toolkit for machine perception. Its hand landmarker finds 21 points on each hand (the wrist and the joints and tips of every finger), which is what my signs are recognised from. Hydra is a live-coding video synth by Olivia Jack that runs in the browser. Like an analogue modular synth, it chains small functions, such as osc().rotate().out(), and the visuals change as soon as a line is run.

Dissertation

Three languages

My notes for the introduction compare two kinds of collaboration. In traditional creative coding, agency and authorship came from building your own system and defining your own rules, as Casey Reas describes. In vibe coding, agency comes from human intention while the AI executes the code. Is that still collaboration? Between humans there is body language, friction and back-and-forth. Between a human and an AI there is no body language: it is frictionless and one-way, a prompt and an outcome. So the question became: if we reintroduce friction while working with AI, can we feel more agency?

The literature review now explores the intersection of three languages. Human and programming language meet in coding and AI prompting; programming and gestural language in user interfaces; gestural and human language in conversation and collaboration. I drew those intersections to show the gap. I wanted a clear structure, one main book per language: Yule for human language, Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs for programming, and the Cambridge Handbook of Gesture Studies for gesture.

My dissertation draft is titled Invisible Process of Man and Machine: How do we write with our body. It asks how writing code through a self-authored gestural language, instead of through AI prompts, shapes a designer's felt sense of agency, and what happens when friction and the body are brought back into AI-assisted creative coding.
